# Notes — التقسيم السادس للفعل (Verb Voice: Active vs Passive)

## Slide 1 — The two categories

Every verb is either **active (maʿlūm)** or **passive (majhūl)**, depending on whether the doer of the action is mentioned.

- If the doer is stated, the verb is active. "Ḥafiẓa Muḥammadun ad-darsa" — Muhammad memorized the lesson. Muhammad (the doer) is right there in the sentence.
- If the doer is dropped, the verb is passive. "Ḥufiẓa ad-darsu" — The lesson was memorized. No one says *who* memorized it — the doer just isn't mentioned.

So the whole distinction comes down to one question: is the doer named in the sentence or not?

## Slide 2 — Why you'd drop the doer, and what fills its place

Three reasons a speaker leaves the doer out:
1. **They don't know who did it** — "The property was stolen" (no idea who the thief was).
2. **It's obvious who did it** — "The rain was sent down" (everyone knows that's Allah, no need to say it).
3. **Something else is standing in for it** — the sentence structure itself points to a substitute instead.

Once the doer is dropped, something has to take its grammatical place — this stand-in is called the **nāʾib al-fāʿil** (deputy of the doer). Sībawayh's view: only the direct object can properly do this job. The Kufan grammarians disagreed and said other things can substitute too — and the text gives three of those:
- A prepositional phrase can stand in ("when it was dropped into their hands").
- A verbal noun (maṣdar) can stand in ("when the Trumpet is blown").
- An adverb of time or place can stand in ("Ramaḍān was fasted" — the month itself takes the doer's grammatical slot).

Key point either way: whatever substitutes for the doer, the verb's shape has to change from its normal active form to mark that shift.

## Slide 3 — How to actually build the passive past tense

This slide is the mechanical "recipe" for turning a past-tense verb passive. There are five situations, depending on what the verb looks like to start with:

1. **Normal verb** (no extra hamza or tā' at the start, and the middle letter isn't an alif): put a damma on the first letter, a kasra on the second-to-last. Ḍaraba (he hit) → Ḍuriba (he was hit). Doubled verbs like radda (he returned something) go through the same logic before contracting: rudida → rudda.
2. **Verb starting with an extra tā'** (like the taʿallama pattern, verb form V/VI): damma goes on both the first and second letters. Taʿallama → Tuʿullima.
3. **Verb starting with hamzat al-waṣl** (form VII/VIII/X patterns): damma goes on the first and third letters. Inṭalaqa → Inṭuliqa.
4. **Verb whose middle letter is an alif that came from a yā'** (hollow verbs like qāla, bāʿa): the first letter gets a kasra instead of damma. Qāla → Qīla, Bāʿa → Bīʿa.
5. **Doubled/geminate verbs** (like shadda, madda): damma goes on the first letter. The Kufan grammarians also allowed a kasra here as a dialectal variant.

You don't need to memorize all five as separate rules from scratch — they're really one idea (shift the vowel pattern toward damma-then-kasra) adapted to whatever irregularity the verb already has.

## Slide 4 — The present tense, and a special catch for intransitive verbs

**Present tense passive** is simpler: damma on the first letter, fatḥa on the second-to-last (even if that vowel is only "understood" rather than written, as happens with weak verbs). Yaḍribu → Yuḍrabu. If the letter before last is a long vowel, it turns into an alif instead: Yaqūlu → Yuqālu, Yabīʿu → Yubāʿu.

The trickier point on this slide: **intransitive verbs normally can't be made passive at all** — there's no object for the "deputy" to be. But there's one workaround: you can still passivize an intransitive verb if you attach it to a specific, meaningful time/place adverb, verbal noun, or a preposition it inherently needs — because that phrase can then step in as the deputy doer.

- "Friday was traveled [on]" — the day itself takes the doer's slot.
- "A fine sitting was sat" — the verbal noun does the job.
- "Joy was felt at his arrival" — because فُرِحَ always requires that specific preposition, the prepositional phrase can substitute.

What doesn't work: a fixed expression like ʿinda, idhā, subḥāna, or maʿādha — these don't flex to take a deputy, so verbs built with them just never go passive.
